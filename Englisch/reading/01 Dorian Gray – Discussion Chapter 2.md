## Chapter 2 at a glance

### Setting
- Basil Hallward's studio in London and the garden behind it, on a hot summer day. Lilacs, roses and bees in the garden.
- It's the last sitting for the portrait.

### Characters
- **Dorian Gray**: about twenty, extremely beautiful, innocent and easily impressed. Before this chapter he has never really thought about himself.
- **Lord Henry Wotton ("Harry")**: witty aristocrat who loves shocking ideas and paradoxes. He wants to see what his words do to Dorian.
- **Basil Hallward**: the painter. He adores Dorian and sees him as the inspiration for his art. In Chapter 1 he asked Henry not to influence Dorian.
- Mentioned: **Lady Agatha**, Henry's aunt. Dorian helps her with charity work in the East End (Whitechapel).

### What happens
1. Dorian arrives and is playing Schumann on the piano when he meets Lord Henry. Basil wants Henry to leave, but Dorian asks him to stay.
2. While Dorian poses, Henry talks about influence and self-development. He says influence is immoral, that people are afraid of themselves, and that "the only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it."
3. Dorian is deeply shaken. He feels completely new thoughts, but they seem to come from himself.
4. Dorian goes into the garden and Henry follows him. Henry gives his big speech about youth: beauty fades, "Time is jealous of you", so live now and try out everything ("a new Hedonism"). Dorian is frightened and fascinated.
5. They go back in and Basil finishes the portrait. When Dorian sees it, he recognises his own beauty for the first time, "like a revelation."
6. Because of Henry's words, the joy turns into pain. Dorian becomes jealous of the painting and makes his wish: the picture should grow old and he should stay young. He says he would give his soul for it.
7. Basil blames Henry. When Basil takes a palette knife to destroy the painting, Dorian stops him: "It would be murder!" Basil gives Dorian the portrait.
8. Henry invites Dorian to the theatre that evening. Dorian goes with Henry instead of having dinner with Basil. Basil stays behind, hurt, with the painting. He says he'll stay "with the real Dorian."

### Key quotes
- "The aim of life is self-development." (Henry)
- "The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it." (Henry)
- "Nothing can cure the soul but the senses, just as nothing can cure the senses but the soul." (Henry)
- "Live! Live the wonderful life that is in you!" (Henry)
- "I am jealous of everything whose beauty does not die." (Dorian)
- "I would give my soul for that!" (Dorian)
- "It would be murder!" (Dorian)

### Themes
- **Influence and manipulation**: Henry changes Dorian with words alone.
- **Youth and beauty**: they're shown as the most valuable things in life, and they don't last.
- **Art vs. life**: the painting stays the same while the real person changes.
- **Hedonism**: living for pleasure and new experiences.
- **The soul**: Dorian's wish is a kind of Faustian pact.

### Why the chapter matters
- It's the turning point of the book. Dorian's innocence ends here.
- Dorian's wish sets up the whole plot, because later the portrait ages and changes instead of him.
- Dorian's choice between Basil and Henry at the end shows which influence will win.

## 1. Pursuing your desires vs. society's expectations

> Lord Henry suggests that people should pursue their desires rather than conform to society's expectations. How might this philosophy influence the choices a young person makes today?

"Lord Henry tells Dorian that 'the aim of life is self-development' and that 'the only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it.' I think this would hit young people today really hard, because it sounds a lot like 'YOLO' or 'just do what makes you happy,' which we hear all the time on social media.

There's a good side to it. It could give someone the courage to pick the career they actually want instead of the one their parents want, or to be honest about who they are. The danger is that Lord Henry never talks about consequences or about other people. If you only follow your desires, you can end up selfish, reckless, or living just for pleasure and looks, and that's exactly where Dorian ends up. What's also interesting is that Lord Henry doesn't live this way himself. In Chapter 1, Basil says he 'never says a moral thing' and 'never does a wrong thing.' So he gives out dangerous advice without taking any of the risks."

## 2. Pop-culture figures like Lord Henry

> Are there any figures in popular culture whom you would compare Lord Henry to? Why?

"The first one I thought of is Tyler Durden from *Fight Club*. He's charismatic and witty, and he talks a younger, insecure man into rejecting society's rules, and the narrator's life falls apart because of it. That's almost the same dynamic as Henry and Dorian.

Another one is Mephistopheles from the Faust story. He's the devil who tempts someone into trading his soul, and Dorian literally says 'I would give my soul' for eternal youth in this chapter.

You could also compare Henry to some influencers. They sell a glamorous lifestyle built around looks, youth and pleasure, they sound confident and clever, and young followers take it in without questioning it. Like Henry, they're very good with words, but they don't take responsibility for what happens to the people who listen to them."

## 3. How Lord Henry uses language to influence Dorian

> How does Lord Henry use language and conversation to influence Dorian? Identify specific examples of his persuasive techniques and discuss why they might be effective.

"Henry hardly ever argues logically. He persuades through style. Here are the techniques I noticed:

- **Paradoxes and witty one-liners.** For example, 'Nothing can cure the soul but the senses, just as nothing can cure the senses but the soul.' They sound deep and shocking, so Dorian feels like he's hearing secret truths.
- **Flattery.** He keeps praising Dorian's beauty and calls him 'a wonderful creation.'
- **Fear and urgency.** He warns that 'Time is jealous of you,' that his beauty will fade, and that 'youth is the one thing worth having.' This makes Dorian panic about getting old.
- **Commands and exclamations.** 'Live! Live the wonderful life that is in you!' It feels like a speech, not a conversation.
- **Timing and setting.** He talks to Dorian alone in the garden with the lilacs, which is a very sensual, emotional moment, and he speaks while Dorian is bored and restless from posing.

These tricks work because Dorian is young and naive and has never really thought about himself before. The best part is that Wilde says the new influences 'seemed to him to have come really from himself.' So Dorian thinks these are his own thoughts, and that's what makes the manipulation so powerful. Dorian even thinks, 'Words! Mere words! How terrible they were!'"

## 4. The portrait as a symbol

> The portrait becomes an important symbol in the novel. Analyse what it represents at this point in the story and consider why Dorian's attitude towards it changes.

"At this point the portrait stands for Dorian's youth and beauty frozen in time, and also for Basil's admiration of him, because Basil put so much of himself into it. When Dorian sees it, 'the sense of his own beauty came on him like a revelation.' It's like a mirror that shows him who he is for the first time.

His attitude changes very quickly. At first he's bored and doesn't care about the sitting. Then he's amazed by the painting. Then, because of what Henry said about ageing, he becomes jealous and bitter: 'I am jealous of everything whose beauty does not die.' The portrait now reminds him that he will grow old and it won't. That's when he makes his wish: 'If it were I who was to be always young, and the picture that was to grow old… I would give my soul for that!'

By the end of the chapter, when Basil tries to destroy it, Dorian shouts 'It would be murder!' So he already sees the portrait as part of himself. This foreshadows that it will become a picture of his soul or conscience."

## 5. Is Basil a worse influence than Lord Henry?

> Can the argument be made that Basil is a more negative influence on Dorian than Lord Henry? Provide supporting evidence for your answer.

"You can make an argument for it. Basil basically worships Dorian and treats his beauty as the most important thing about him. He's the one who paints the portrait, and without it Dorian would never have made the wish. He also brings Dorian and Henry together even though he knew Henry was dangerous, and then he doesn't really protect Dorian.

But I don't think Basil is the worse influence. He genuinely cares about Dorian. He even asked Henry not to spoil him, and he tries to destroy the painting once he sees it's causing harm. He tells Dorian, 'I have never had such a friend as you.' Henry is the one who deliberately plants ideas in Dorian's head, and he enjoys watching the effect his words have. So Basil's influence is accidental and comes from love, while Henry's is on purpose and almost experimental. That makes Henry more negative in my view, but Basil isn't completely innocent either."
