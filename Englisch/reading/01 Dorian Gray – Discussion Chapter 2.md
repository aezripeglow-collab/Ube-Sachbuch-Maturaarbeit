## Meine Antwort (zum Vorlesen)

### Intro: What happens in Chapter 2

Before I start with the questions, I'll quickly say what happens in Chapter 2, because I think it's important for all of our answers. The whole chapter takes place in Basil's studio and in the garden behind it on a really hot summer day, and it's actually the last sitting for the portrait. Dorian meets Lord Henry there for the first time. Basil actually wanted Henry to leave, but Dorian asked him to stay.

While Dorian is posing, Henry talks to him about influence and about living your life to the fullest, and Dorian gets really shaken by it. Then in the garden Henry gives this big speech about youth and about how beauty doesn't last. When Basil finishes the painting, Dorian sees his own beauty for the first time, but because of what Henry said, he gets jealous of the portrait and wishes that the picture would grow old instead of him. He even says he would give his soul for that. At the end Basil wants to destroy the painting, Dorian stops him, and then Dorian goes to the theatre with Henry instead of having dinner with Basil. For me this is the chapter where Dorian's innocence really ends. I'll now go through the questions one by one.

### 1. Pursuing your desires vs. society's expectations

> Lord Henry suggests that people should pursue their desires rather than conform to society's expectations. How might this philosophy influence the choices a young person makes today?

I'll start with the first question, about following your desires instead of what society expects. Henry tells Dorian that "the aim of life is self-development" and that "the only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it." When I read this, I noticed that it actually sounds a lot like things we hear all the time on social media today, for example "YOLO" or "just do what makes you happy." So I think a young person today could be influenced by this philosophy quite easily.

I have to say that I don't think it's only negative. It could give someone the courage to choose the career they really want instead of the one their parents want, or to be honest about who they are. But what I find problematic is that Henry never talks about consequences or about other people. If you only follow your desires, you can become really selfish or reckless, and that's exactly what happens to Dorian later in the book.

What also surprised me is that Henry himself doesn't really live like this. In Chapter 1, Basil says that Henry "never says a moral thing" and "never does a wrong thing." So Henry gives this advice to a young person without taking any of the risks himself, and I think that's important to keep in mind.

### 2. Pop-culture figures like Lord Henry

> Are there any figures in popular culture whom you would compare Lord Henry to? Why?

For the second question I thought about who in pop culture is similar to Lord Henry. The first one that came to my mind is Tyler Durden from *Fight Club*, because he is charming and clever and he talks a younger, insecure man into breaking society's rules, and in the end the narrator's life falls apart because of it. I think that's very close to what happens between Henry and Dorian.

Another one is Mephistopheles from the Faust story, so the devil who tempts someone into trading his soul. This fits especially well with Chapter 2, because Dorian really says "I would give my soul for that" when he wishes to stay young.

I would also compare Henry to some influencers, not to one specific person but more to the type. They show a lifestyle where looks, youth and pleasure are the most important things, they sound very sure of themselves, and young followers often just believe them without thinking about it. Like Henry, they are good with words, but they don't take responsibility for what happens to the people who listen to them.

### 3. How Lord Henry uses language to influence Dorian

> How does Lord Henry use language and conversation to influence Dorian? Identify specific examples of his persuasive techniques and discuss why they might be effective.

Now to the third question, how Henry uses language to influence Dorian. The first thing I noticed is that Henry almost never argues logically. He convinces Dorian more with the way he talks than with real arguments. I found a few techniques.

First, he uses paradoxes and clever sentences, for example "Nothing can cure the soul but the senses, just as nothing can cure the senses but the soul." Sentences like this sound really deep, so Dorian has the feeling that he's hearing some kind of secret truth.

Second, he flatters Dorian and keeps talking about his beauty. He even calls him "a wonderful creation."

Third, he makes Dorian afraid. He tells him that "Time is jealous of you" and that youth is the only thing worth having, which makes Dorian panic about getting old.

Fourth, he uses commands and exclamations like "Live! Live the wonderful life that is in you!", so it sounds more like a speech than a normal conversation.

I would also add the timing and the setting. Dorian is bored and restless from posing, and Henry talks to him alone in the garden between the lilacs, so it's a very emotional moment for him.

I think these techniques work so well because Dorian is young and naive and has never really thought about himself before. What I find especially interesting is that Wilde writes that the new influences "seemed to him to have come really from himself." So Dorian thinks these are his own thoughts, and that's why the manipulation is so strong.

### 4. The portrait as a symbol

> The portrait becomes an important symbol in the novel. Analyse what it represents at this point in the story and consider why Dorian's attitude towards it changes.

For the fourth question, about the portrait as a symbol: at this point in the story I think the portrait stands for Dorian's youth and beauty, kept forever in the painting. It also shows how much Basil admires him, because Basil put a lot of himself into this painting. When Dorian sees it, Wilde writes that "the sense of his own beauty came on him like a revelation," so it's a bit like a mirror where he sees himself properly for the first time.

What surprised me is how fast his attitude changes. At the beginning he is bored and doesn't really care about the sitting. Then he is amazed when he sees the painting. And then, because of what Henry told him about getting old, he becomes jealous and sad. He says "I am jealous of everything whose beauty does not die," and that's when he makes his wish that the picture should grow old instead of him. So for me the reason for the change is mainly Henry's words, because without the speech in the garden Dorian would probably just have been happy about the painting.

I also have to add the end of the chapter. When Basil wants to destroy the painting with a knife, Dorian shouts "It would be murder!" This shows me that he already sees the portrait as a part of himself, and I think it's already a hint that later the portrait will show what happens to his soul.

### 5. Is Basil a worse influence than Lord Henry?

> Can the argument be made that Basil is a more negative influence on Dorian than Lord Henry? Provide supporting evidence for your answer.

The last question is whether Basil is a more negative influence than Henry. I think you can actually make an argument for it. Basil admires Dorian so much that he almost worships him, and he treats Dorian's beauty as the most important thing about him. He also painted the portrait, and without the portrait Dorian would never have made his wish. And he brought Dorian and Henry together, even though he knew that Henry could be dangerous, and then he didn't really protect Dorian from him.

But in the end I still think Henry is the worse influence. Basil really cares about Dorian. He asked Henry not to influence him, he even tries to destroy the painting when he sees that it's hurting Dorian, and he tells him "I have never had such a friend as you." Henry, on the other hand, puts his ideas into Dorian's head on purpose and enjoys seeing what his words do to him. So I would say that Basil's influence happens more by accident and comes from caring about Dorian, and Henry's influence is on purpose. That's why I think Henry is more negative, but Basil isn't completely innocent either.

That's everything from my side. I'm curious what you think, especially about the last question.

## Chapter 2 at a glance

### Setting
- Basil Hallward's studio in London and the garden behind it, on a hot summer day. Lilacs, roses and bees in the garden.
- It's the last sitting for the portrait.

### Characters
- **Dorian Gray**: about twenty, extremely beautiful, innocent and easily impressed. Before this chapter he has never really thought about himself.
- **Lord Henry Wotton ("Harry")**: witty aristocrat who loves shocking ideas and paradoxes. He wants to see what his words do to Dorian.
- **Basil Hallward**: the painter. He adores Dorian and sees him as the inspiration for his art. In Chapter 1 he asked Henry not to influence Dorian.
- Mentioned: **Lady Agatha**, Henry's aunt. Dorian helps her with charity work in the East End (Whitechapel).

### What happens
1. Dorian arrives and is playing Schumann on the piano when he meets Lord Henry. Basil wants Henry to leave, but Dorian asks him to stay.
2. While Dorian poses, Henry talks about influence and self-development. He says influence is immoral, that people are afraid of themselves, and that "the only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it."
3. Dorian is deeply shaken. He feels completely new thoughts, but they seem to come from himself.
4. Dorian goes into the garden and Henry follows him. Henry gives his big speech about youth: beauty fades, "Time is jealous of you", so live now and try out everything ("a new Hedonism"). Dorian is frightened and fascinated.
5. They go back in and Basil finishes the portrait. When Dorian sees it, he recognises his own beauty for the first time, "like a revelation."
6. Because of Henry's words, the joy turns into pain. Dorian becomes jealous of the painting and makes his wish: the picture should grow old and he should stay young. He says he would give his soul for it.
7. Basil blames Henry. When Basil takes a palette knife to destroy the painting, Dorian stops him: "It would be murder!" Basil gives Dorian the portrait.
8. Henry invites Dorian to the theatre that evening. Dorian goes with Henry instead of having dinner with Basil. Basil stays behind, hurt, with the painting. He says he'll stay "with the real Dorian."

### Key quotes
- "The aim of life is self-development." (Henry)
- "The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it." (Henry)
- "Nothing can cure the soul but the senses, just as nothing can cure the senses but the soul." (Henry)
- "Live! Live the wonderful life that is in you!" (Henry)
- "I am jealous of everything whose beauty does not die." (Dorian)
- "I would give my soul for that!" (Dorian)
- "It would be murder!" (Dorian)

### Themes
- **Influence and manipulation**: Henry changes Dorian with words alone.
- **Youth and beauty**: they're shown as the most valuable things in life, and they don't last.
- **Art vs. life**: the painting stays the same while the real person changes.
- **Hedonism**: living for pleasure and new experiences.
- **The soul**: Dorian's wish is a kind of Faustian pact.

### Why the chapter matters
- It's the turning point of the book. Dorian's innocence ends here.
- Dorian's wish sets up the whole plot, because later the portrait ages and changes instead of him.
- Dorian's choice between Basil and Henry at the end shows which influence will win.
